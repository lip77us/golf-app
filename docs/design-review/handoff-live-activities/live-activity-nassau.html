Nassau on the lock screen

Nassau is the second Live Activity, and it breaks the composition the first one was built on. Sixes had one match at a time, so it got one big number. Nassau always has two live at once — the nine you are playing and the eighteen — and there is no honest way to pick which of them is the headline.

So the card is two equal rows. Nothing is 36px any more; both numbers sit at 25px, which is still the largest thing on the panel and still readable in a pocket-glance.

The sides are named once, at the top

The one thing Nassau gives back. In Sixes the pairing changes every match, so both sides had to be re-stated on every update — that line was the whole design problem. Nassau’s sides are fixed at setup and never move, so they are named once, above the matches, and the space goes to the scores instead.

It also means colour is stable for the whole round: blue is blue on the 1st and on the 18th, in both matches. A number can wear its side’s colour and the reader never has to re-learn it.

Two rows, and the current nine goes first

FRONT 9 then OVERALL; after the turn, BACK 9 then OVERALL. The nine leads because it settles sooner and it is the one that changes character — the overall match drifts, the nine gets urgent.

The settled nine does not stay on the card as a third row. It is over, its money is in the footer figure, and a row that cannot change is a row spending space on history. What it leaves behind is the money and nothing else.

The hole, not the thru count

Sixes counted holes played. Nassau names the hole you are standing on: HOLE 12. Two live matches means two different remaining-hole counts, so a single thru figure no longer does the work it did — and the hole number is what a group actually says out loud.

Each row keeps its own n TO PLAY, which is the honest way to carry two clocks at once: 6 to play on the back nine, 6 to play on the overall, and they diverge the moment a nine closes out.

The exposure range replaces the running total

−$10 to +$20. Two numbers: the worst and best places this round can finish, given what is settled and what is still live. It is the answer to the question people actually have on the 14th, which is not where am I but how bad can this get.

A single running figure cannot answer it in Nassau, because presses mean the amount at stake is not fixed at setup. Three open bets at $5 is a $30 swing; the same round after two presses is a $50 swing, and the running total looks identical in both.

The range includes settled money, so it is a real forecast rather than a bracket around zero — win the front nine and the floor rises with you. And it converges: every bet that settles pulls the two ends together, until on the 18th green they meet at the single number that is the final state. Same slot all round, no special final treatment needed.

All three bets exist from the 1st tee. The back nine is not played yet but it is already at stake, so the range opens at −$15 to +$15 on a $5 Nassau — not −$10, which would count only the matches under way. This is the correction that makes the whole slot honest: the range is about money at risk, and the back nine is at risk from the moment the round starts.

The arithmetic, exactly: settled ± the sum of every live stake, presses included. Midpoint is the money already banked; half-span is what is still on the table. Both ends move on every settlement and every press, and they are checkable against the rows — which is worth saying because a range that contradicts the scores above it is worse than no range at all.

Presses are the news

A press is called by a person, mid-round, and it changes what the round is worth. That is exactly the shape of thing that earns a push — the same test Sixes applied to the draw.

Dave pressed the back nine. The activity underneath is already showing it: the row carries a +1 PRESS chip and the exposure range has widened. No banner, no CTA, no accept button — a press is not a negotiation on a lock screen, and a mis-tap there is expensive.

The second push is a nine closing out — Front nine to blue, 3&2. Money changed hands and one of the two rows disappeared, which is the only other event in Nassau nobody can infer from the hole they are standing on.

Where the press chip lives

On the row it belongs to, not in the header. A press is a bet on one match, and floating the count up to the top would say the round has presses without saying which match carries them — which is the whole content.

Orange rather than mint, because a press is not neutral: someone did it. Where a match has two, it reads +2 PRESS and not two chips.

2v2 is the same card

Four names on one line instead of two, shrink-to-fit, and every other slot identical. Worth checking on the narrowest phone: Paul Kelly & Dave Moran v. Sam Reid & Lee Naylor is the longest string this design has to hold, and it is the reason the sides line got the full width.

Late on, the card has one row

Both nines settled and only the eighteen still live: the two-row composition collapses to one. It is not a degraded state, it is the only point in the round where the floor is positive — $10 banked against a single $5 bet, so the range reads +$5 to +$15 and the round cannot be lost.

Worth knowing it exists, because a card built to hold two rows has to look deliberate holding one. The row keeps its size; nothing stretches to fill the gap.

Always-on and the eight hours

Same view, held back. A 25px number survives the brightness pull where a stack of 13px rows would not — which is the argument for having spent the card’s space on two numbers and nothing else.

Starts on the first score posted, same as Sixes, for the same reason: it buys back the range and the car park against the eight-hour limit, and an abandoned round leaves no ghost.

The last state is the collection

The range has converged, so the card says the one thing left: what you won, and who to see. Holds a few minutes and dismisses itself.
