Skins on the lock screen

Skins gets the one big number back. Nassau needed two rows because it always has two matches live; skins has one thing that pays — a hole — so the composition Sixes was built on returns intact.

Which hole is the whole question, and the two games answer it differently. Single group: the hole in front of you, worth whatever has carried into it, the only figure that changes how the next tee shot gets played. Multi-group: the golfer who just won a hole, somewhere out on the course, the moment the last group closed it. A name, not a sum — multi-group is one pot, and what a skin is worth is not known until the last one has been taken. The counts sit underneath, at stat-line size.

Two games, four shapes, and the title says which

Skins is run two ways and they are different games. A single-group game is four players and one pot — the whole competition is the people you are walking with, and a hole is decided the moment the four of you have played it. A multi-group game scores the same hole across the field: your 3 has to beat all sixteen cards, so nothing is decided until every group has been through.

The header names it outright, because the two cards go on to say different things and the reader has to know which one he is holding:

SKINS · …Single group. Four players, decided the moment the four of them have played the hole. The word after the dot names the variation — CARRYOVERS, POOL, POOL + JUNK — and the card is personal.
MULTI-GROUP SKINSField-scored, one pot, no carries. A hole closes when the last group clears it, and the card reports those closures rather than the reader.

It sits next to the mark on every lock-screen state, so a reader never has to infer which game he is looking at from the numbers. The Dynamic Island is the one exception and it has to be: the compact pill has room for the pot and one qualifier, so the name waits for the expanded state. The signal is the presence or absence of MULTI-GROUP; anything starting SKINS · is the game in front of you. The word does the work, not a badge and not a group number. Which group you are in is not on the player card at all — you know it, and every closure it reports belongs to the field rather than to any one group. A group number appears nowhere: the player card reports the field, and a watcher gets that same card.

Three ways the single-group game is played

Single group is the common case, so it leads the sheet. It comes in three shapes, and they change what the number on the card means:

Carryovers$2 a player, a skin a hole. A tied hole carries into the next one, so the pot in the headline is this hole’s money and it grows every time nobody wins. Six holes of ties makes the 17th worth $36.
PoolOne ante, one pot. The headline is how many skins have been won and what one is worth right now. The pot itself is a footer stat.
Pool + junkOne pot, two ways to earn from it. Junk — birdies, sandies, greenies — is worth exactly what a skin is worth, and divides the same pot. Four skins and two junk is six shares.

Multi-group is a fourth shape and gets its own composition further down, because it is the only one where a hole is not decided in front of you.

Carryovers: the pot is the headline until it is won

While the carry is alive the number is the money on this hole — $36 on the 17th, six skins deep. That is the figure that changes how the tee shot gets played, and it belongs at 36px.

The moment somebody takes it, the news is not the next pot. It is who just got paid: S. Reid — won $36 on the 17th. A six-hole carry is six skins to whoever takes it, so the footer turns over in the same instant — 5 skins · S. Reid leads — 6. The name takes the headline, the amount sits beside it, and the 18th drops to the sub-line where it belongs — $6 on the line on the 18th, no carry. Six dollars after thirty-six is a footnote, and treating it as the headline would misread the moment badly.

The same slots, reordered by what just happened. A running card leads with money at stake; a card one minute after a payout leads with the golfer who took it.

What a non-leader sees

The slot is the same shape for everybody, and only the second line changes. The leader gets YOU LEAD; everyone else gets the name in front of them and the gap — 2 SKINS · M. ALVAREZ +3.

A gap, not a placing. Third of four is a rank and tells a golfer nothing he can act on, whereas +3 is the number of holes he has to take to draw level, and with a $36 carry sitting on the 17th it can close in one swing.

The leader is named because in a four-man group he is a person, not a position. When two are level the most recent winner holds the line, so the chaser reads M. ALVAREZ +3 and not a tie he has to unpick.

A watcher has no count of his own, so the slot names the leader outright — M. ALVAREZ · LEADS · 5 — and the footer trades the gross and the money for par 5 · 11 settled and the field size. Three readings of one slot: your lead, your gap, or the leader.

This is where single group differs from multi-group, where the watcher card and the player card are identical. Here the player card is personal, so the watcher version has something to strip.

Pool leads with the divisor and the price

Per-skin pays out every time a hole is won. Pool antes once and divides that single pot among however many skins get won by the end, so the two figures that matter are how many have gone and what one is worth now: 4 SKINS · $15 a skin now.

The word now carries what a longer phrase used to. An earlier draft read each, and falling, which spent the slot explaining a mechanism the reader already understands — four skins into a $60 pot is $15 apiece and a fifth makes it $12. Now says the same thing in three letters and lets the counts do the rest.

The pot moves to the footer, where it belongs: $60 is fixed at the ante and never changes, which makes it a stat rather than news. What changes is the divisor above it.

Sam Reid took the 6th gets a line to itself. In pool a skin is everybody else’s share dropping, so the golfer responsible is named, and crowding that onto the end of a count line buried the one human fact on the card.

It persists. The line still reads Sam Reid took the 6th on the 7th, the 8th and the 9th if nobody wins in between — the last skin taken is the standing state of the game, not a flash that expires. A card that blanked the line on the next tee would be hiding the answer to the question it raises.

The footer reads across the whole game — $60 pot · 1 skin · $15 ante — and the money slot nets it: one skin of four is $15 back against a $15 ante, so $0. Pool does not pay as it goes, so that figure moves every time anyone wins a hole.

The watcher keeps both headline figures, since neither is personal, and trades the reader’s skin count and money for par and the field size. It is drawn on the next hole to show the persistence: HOLE 8, and Sam Reid still holds the 6th.

Junk is a skin by another name

It is one pot, not two. A junk point — a birdie, a sandy, a greenie — is worth exactly what a skin is worth, and both divide the same money. 4 skins and 2 junk is six shares, no different from six skins.

So both counts take the headline, side by side: 4 SKINS · 2 JUNK, with the price underneath — $10 each now. A $60 pot over six shares is $10, and it falls the same way for either kind of earning. Burying junk in the sub-line, as an earlier draft did, made it look like a side pot and understated what the round is dividing.

The named line carries both: Sam Reid took the 6th — 1 skin, 1 junk. One hole can produce two shares, which is the thing about this variation a reader has to see once to understand.

The footer holds the pot and the reader’s own holdings across both kinds — $60 pot · 1 skin · 1 junk — and the money slot nets them: two shares at $10 against a $15 ante, +$5.

The multi-group card shows settled holes, not the hole you are on

A multi-group skin is not decided until every group has played that hole, and there are no carries — a hole nobody wins is simply halved and produces no skin.

An earlier draft put the hole you were standing on in the headline and marked it PROVISIONAL. That solved the wrong problem. A scorer knows what hole he is on, and his own position is on the leaderboard and on the entry screen he opens at least once a hole. What he cannot see is a hole finally closing out behind him.

So the card leads with the event and files the counts underneath it:

SAM REID
HOLE 9		The headline. A golfer beat fifteen cards on a hole and the money moved. That is news; a running total never is.

5 halved		The stat line. Under the headline, at stat-line size. Most holes in a sixteen-golfer field get tied, so the awarded count stays small all afternoon and reads badly as a headline.
FULL FIELD THRU 9The boundary. In the header, because it is context rather than news. Four awarded plus five halved is the nine the field is through.
5 leadersBottom left. Open holes where one golfer already holds the low score outright — the skins about to happen.

Nothing here is a forecast, which is why the number is mint on every state and the word PROVISIONAL has left the design. 5 leaders comes closest, but it states a fact about scores already posted — five open holes currently have a sole low score standing — not a prediction about who will win them.

Groups go out in order, so settled holes are a contiguous block behind the last group on the course — not a scatter. That is why the header reads FULL FIELD THRU 9 rather than a bare count: it names the boundary, and the reader subtracts his own hole number from it to see how far ahead of the settlement he is standing. On the 12th with the field through 9, three of his own holes are still open.

Ties are the normal case. Sixteen net cards on a par 4 produce a lot of shared low scores, so halved holes outnumber skins most of the day — five to four here. That is the strongest argument for demoting the awarded count: a number that spends the round in single figures, moving one at a time, cannot carry a lock screen.

The single-group game keeps the older composition, because it does not have the problem: four players, one card each, the hole settles when they hole out. It shows the pot in front of them, carries and all.

The multi-group card is not about you

This is the decision that shapes the whole composition. In a sixteen-golfer game a golfer already knows his own round — the leaderboard has it, and the entry screen is open at least once a hole. Repeating gross, standing and money on the lock screen spends the best surface in the app on the one thing the reader cannot fail to know.

What he cannot see is the rest of the course. Fifteen other cards are being posted somewhere he is not, and holes are closing out behind him all afternoon. So the card reports those closures:

HeadlineThe golfer who just won a hole, and which hole. No score: it is over.
Stat lineSkins awarded and holes halved. The state of the game, not the state of you.
FooterOpen holes with a leader, and who leads on skins.

4 skins awarded · 5 halved completes the arithmetic against the header: the two add to the nine holes the field is through. Halves make the surviving skins worth more, so a reader holding one wants the halved count high and the awarded count low.

The single-group card keeps the personal read, because there is no field to report — three opponents, all of them within earshot. It says YOU LEAD rather than a placing: 1st of 4 reads like a finishing position in a stroke-play field, when all it ever meant was that nobody in the group has more skins. Its state slot holds 5 SKINS · YOU LEAD and its footer keeps the stake and the count.

The round-complete card is not personal either. It reports the result of the game, which is what the whole field and every watcher are waiting for at the same moment.

No dollar figure on a hole, and no score

The pot is one pot, divided at the end among however many skins get won, so what a hole paid cannot be stated while the round is running. Four skins into $192 is $48 apiece; six makes it $32. Printing a number against hole 9 would be a guess dressed as a receipt.

So the headline names the winner and not a figure. The awarded count is the divisor — the one number that moves everybody’s money at once — and it belongs in the stat line where it can be read against the halved count beside it.

The winning score is gone too. A net 3 on the 9th tells the reader nothing he can use: the hole is closed, and nobody is chasing it. The name and the hole are the news; everything else was padding.

The winner is named, and that is new

Every earlier draft kept names off the card: in skins the field is the opponent, and naming it would be a list. One settled hole has exactly one name, so the objection disappears. It is the headline now, with the hole beside it: SAM REID · HOLE 9 · WON THE SKIN.

The footer names one more, and only one: D. Kerr leads · 2 skins. A count, no score beside it, and the word skins spelled out because two of anything is ambiguous on a lock screen. Ties resolve to the most recent winner — a second skin for M. Alvarez and the slot reads M. Alvarez leads · 2 skins.

The single-group card still names nobody. Three opponents standing next to you do not need announcing.

A hole closing out is the news

Hole 9 goes to Sam Reid. The trigger is the last group coming through, which is the only moment anything in this game becomes true. It fires wherever the reader is on the course, and it is the event the card is built around.

The second line places it in the golfer’s day: His 1st of the day. Nobody matched it. At most eighteen of these a round, far fewer once halved holes are counted, and every one is a share of the pot moving in a group the reader cannot see.

Halved holes are announced too, in the same slot and without ceremony — Hole 10 was halved. No skin, three golfers tied at net 4. With no carries that is a real result, and in a pool it is good news for everyone holding a skin: one fewer way to divide the money. The card takes the same shape with the name slot greyed to HALVED, so the two outcomes read as one series rather than an event and an absence.

ActivityAmbient. The last hole to close, always current.
NotificationPunctual. Fires when the last group clears a hole.
Your ownSame event, different voice — Hole 11 is yours.
Never bothThe activity does not flash. It is already showing the hole the push announced.

Multi-group watchers get the same card

A watcher is following a round they are not playing — a spouse, a TD, a golfer waiting on the tee. In the multi-group game they get the player card unchanged, and there is no separate design for them. That is the strongest evidence the framing is right: once the personal figures came off, there was nothing left to strip.

The round-complete card is the same for both, for the same reason. A watcher wants the result, and so does everybody who played.

Gross leads the single-group footer

Single-group only: +6 · $2 a player · 11 settled. Gross is the number a golfer checks without meaning to, and it earns its place because par is in that card’s header — net skins turn on strokes, so par 5 and +6 together say what the round has done to his chances on this one.

Settled counts skins, not holes: with the 12th onward still in the pot, sixteen holes are behind him and only eleven have paid anybody.

The multi-group footer gives that space to the field instead — 5 leaders on open holes, and who leads on skins.

Money is settled, and only where it belongs

It sits on the single-group cards and nowhere else — not on multi-group, and not on round complete, which resolves the pool’s per-skin value rather than anybody’s balance. +$18 after five skins: five collected at $6, six settled skins costing $2 each. Both directions, or it is not your position — a gross figure of skins won reads like a win when you are down.

The multi-group card carries no running total, and in a pool it could not: a skin is worth $48 or $32 depending on holes nobody has played yet. That is what the stat line and the leaders count are for — the direction the divisor is moving. The round-complete card is where it resolves.

Round complete is the result, not a receipt

It is the one card everybody wants at the same time — every golfer in the field, and every watcher — so it reports the game, not the reader.

Two things become true only at the last hole, and the card gives both real weight. Round complete leads, in the display face at 21px — the only card in the set that opens with a sentence rather than a value, because the fact that it is over is the news. Under it, what a skin was worth: $32 a skin, 6 won from a $192 pot. That figure was a moving target all afternoon; here it is finally a number.

The header slot gives up its hole context, since there is no hole any more, and holds the shape of the game instead: 18 HOLES · 16 GOLFERS.

Then the winners, on a row of their own so no name is ever split from its count: Winners: D. Kerr 2 · M. Alvarez 2 · S. Reid 1 · T. Ochoa 1. Six skins between four golfers, and two of them tied on two. No ranking and no tiebreak — in a pool there is no overall winner, only golfers holding skins.

The label is the plain word Winners: and every golfer is named the same way, the reader included. Nothing on this card says you — it is the group’s result and the watchers’, and a card that personalises one line of it reads differently to each person looking at it, which is the opposite of what a shared result should do.

The footer takes the round’s dimensions, which no longer need to be inferred from anywhere else: 16 golfers · $192 pot · 12 halved. Those three give the whole arithmetic — the ante, the divisor, and why the divisor stayed small.

Twelve holes nobody took is why the six that landed were worth $32 instead of $11, which is the fact the whole afternoon turned on.

Par, and what replaced it

HOLE 17 · PAR 5 on the single-group card. Skins is the one game where par belongs on a lock screen — net skins turn on strokes, and whether a 5 is good enough is the question the pot is asking.

The multi-group header has no par and no hole, because it is not describing a hole anybody is about to play. It names the boundary instead: FULL FIELD THRU 9.
