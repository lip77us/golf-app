Sixes on the lock screen

Sixes is the right first Live Activity, and not because it is the simplest. It is the one game where something changes that you cannot know without being told: the pairing changes every time a match is decided, and it is drawn rather than scheduled.

Everything else the app could put on a lock screen — your score, the money — you already know, because you were there when it happened. Sixes has news.

It is a scoreboard, not a personal report

2 UP, with both pairings named and the leader bold. Not you are 2 up.

Four golfers see the same string, which matters more than it sounds: the Sixes pairing changes every match, so a phone that says 2 UP without saying who is unreadable forty minutes later. Naming both sides fixes the partner problem and the perspective problem in one line, and it means two golfers can look at one phone.

The number wears the leading team’s colour, and so does their name. Mint said nothing — it is the app’s colour, not a side’s, so a mint 2 UP above a blue row and an orange row leaves the reader guessing which one is up. Blue number, blue name, the other side dimmed: the tie between the score and the side is carried by colour rather than by reading order, which nobody trusts.

The right-hand slot is the state of the match, not the money

An earlier pass put a running balance there under the label yours, and nobody could tell what it meant — yours as opposed to whose? On a neutral board a personal number in the loudest corner is a category error.

It carries DORMIE instead. Two up with two to play is the one fact that changes how the next hole gets played, and it is the word golfers actually say. When no state word applies the slot holds holes remaining in the current match — the match’s nominal block minus what has been played. Counting the round instead was considered and dropped: 2 UP and 14 TO PLAY are answers to different questions, and the pair reads as a contradiction. Both numbers describe the match, and a half-played round with a match on the 5th tee is 3 TO PLAY, not 14. DORMIE is computed the same way — it is a match word and there is nothing else it could mean.

Money moves to the footer, beside the stake, where the context makes the label unnecessary. It is the second question all round and it now reads like one.

Two rules on it. It is settled money only — a match pays when it closes, so the slot is empty until the first match is settled, not $0: an in-progress match is worth nothing yet and a zero implies it was played for nothing. And so far came off, because a figure that only ever counts closed matches does not need the hedge.

The footer carries the gross score

+2 · Thru 4 · $5 a match. Gross leads because it is the number a golfer checks without meaning to, and it is the one personal figure the neutral board can afford — the footer is already the row where the reader’s own round lives, next to the money.

The third slot is always the stake. An earlier pass alternated it with the last match’s result (blue won match 1), which does not survive the format: the teams change every match, so a result naming a side is about a pairing that no longer exists — sitting directly under the current pairing, which shares golfers with it. The stake never goes stale and never contradicts the rows above.

Thru sits in the footer, not on the sides line

The headline band holds three things and two of them cannot shrink, so the pairing line has about a hundred pixels. Sam Reid & Lee Naylor · thru 4 does not fit in it and breaks after “thru”.

The hole count is round context, not a side — it belongs beside the stake in the footer, which already carries that kind of thing. Moving it buys the pairing its own line, which is the slot three rounds of this design have been about.

With the pips gone, that line takes full names. Paul Kelly & Dave Moran settles who is playing with whom for a group that has two Pauls or a guest nobody has met, and the row was always wide enough — it was the stacked elements above and below that made it feel tight.

No segment pips

Three bars for three segments read well in isolation and earned nothing on the phone. The holes-remaining line already answers “how much is left”, and the footer carries the last match result in words when there is one — so the pips were a second, vaguer copy of two things already on the panel.

Then the model settled and cut them for a second reason: a round has no fixed number of matches. A match ends when it is decided and the next pairing starts on the next tee, so three bars would be wrong as often as right. Nothing on this card may be composed as thirds.

Cutting them takes a row out of the middle of the card, which the full names needed. They survive in the expanded Dynamic Island, where there is no footer to carry the same information.

The draw gets no special state

An earlier pass gave the draw its own card with an Open to draw button. That was wrong twice over: a lock-screen button on group state strands the other three, and a Live Activity that asks for something stops being a glance.

So the activity does what it does the rest of the round — it updates. The pairing changes, the two names change, the pip advances. No banner, no CTA, no waiting state.

The notification does the announcing

What the draw actually needs is a push, once, when the pairing lands: New partners — holes 7–12. The nominal range, which stays honest even when the match before it closed out on the 4th and this one started on the 5th — it names the block the group agreed to play, not a claim about what was played. That is a different object with a different job. It arrives, it is read, it is dismissed, and the Live Activity underneath is already correct.

One kind of moment earns one: a new pairing. That is three pushes in a round at minimum — a match ends when it is decided, not when its holes run out, so a round of decisive matches produces more. Nothing else pushes.

ActivityAmbient. Always current, never asks.
NotificationPunctual. Fires on every pairing change, and nothing else.
Never bothThe activity does not also flash. It is already showing the answer the push announced.

Points, not holes, in High-Low

Classic scores a point a hole and reads 2 UP. High-Low scores two — low v low and high v high — and the same phrasing would be a lie. It reads +3 PTS.

The split does not go on the lock screen. An earlier pass showed low +2 · high +1, which is how the points were earned rather than what they are — and a running total is the only thing the match is decided on. The breakdown belongs in the app, on a card that can afford it.

Always-on is the same view, held back

No separate composition. iOS pulls the refresh rate and the brightness down; the activity keeps its structure because the big number is already the only thing that has to survive there. Anything needing a second glance would fail, which is a useful test to apply before adding a line.

Drawn at roughly the real reduction rather than the theoretical one. A near-black mock made the state look broken when it is simply quieter — and the panel’s own contrast, mint and blue on a frosted dark ground, is what carries it.

The quiet alternative

The second treatment drops the frosted panel for a dark slab, shrinks the number, and pulls the mint back to a single accent on the pips. It is what this should look like if it turns out people leave it running for four hours and resent it — the same information, asking for none of the attention.

Worth shipping the loud one and watching. A glanceable thing that nobody glances at is the failure mode, not a thing that is slightly too bright.

Eight hours, and the start moment

iOS ends a Live Activity after eight hours, and a slow round with a turn is close. It starts on the first score posted — which buys back the half hour in the car park and on the range, and means an abandoned round never leaves a ghost on the lock screen.

The last state is not a scoreboard

When the round signs, the neutral board becomes the one personal thing: what you won, and who to see. It holds a few minutes and dismisses itself. Nobody needs a match summary on a lock screen; they need to know who has the cash.

Sixes owns the round

One activity per round. If a foursome is running skins alongside, it is not on here — two activities for one round would be the worst outcome, and Sixes is the one with news.
