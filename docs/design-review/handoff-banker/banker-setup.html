Banker, setting it up

Every other game in the app has a stake you can write on one line. Banker has a band, a per-hole ceiling set by one golfer, and four rules that multiply — and the interaction of those is the only thing this screen really has to communicate.

The band is two numbers and they are not symmetric

The minimum protects the banker: it is what an opponent must have on the hole, so nobody sits out the hole they think they will lose. The maximum protects everyone else: it is the ceiling the banker cannot raise past, whatever he announces on the tee.

Drawn as a two-handle track rather than two fields, because the width of the band is the decision and two boxes hide it. $5 to $50 is a different game from $20 to $25.

Exposure is the headline, not a footnote

This is the one game in the set where a golfer can lose a genuinely surprising amount on a single hole, and it is not because the stakes are high — it is because the banker faces three bets at once and both sides can double them.

Nobody setting up a $50 game is picturing eighteen hundred dollars. The panel shows the ladder rather than one worst case, because the point is not that the top number is likely — it is that the top number exists, and a golfer who reads the second line will set the ceiling differently.

So the screen offers a hole cap, off by default

One number that stops the whole hole — all three bets, all doubles — from exceeding it. This is not part of the traditional game and it is presented as an addition, unticked, with the ladder above it as the argument for ticking it.

It is off by default because the group that plays Banker properly does not want it, and a safety rail switched on without being asked reads as the app deciding the stakes. Shown, argued, and left to them.

Rotation is where the app helps most

The traditional rule is low score, holed out first, and the second half of that is the part an app cannot see. The default is therefore to ask: on a tie the group gets a one-tap picker on the tee and answers from what actually happened on the green. A draw is offered as an alternative for groups who would rather not adjudicate, and a drawn result stays changeable until a score is posted — the app’s guess must never outrank the group’s memory.

The old reasoning, kept because it still applies to the draw option: a phone does not know who putted out first, and guessing silently would be worse than guessing.

So a tie for low score is drawn at random, stated plainly on the row. The alternative — the current banker keeps it — concentrates the role and the exposure with whoever is playing well, which is the opposite of what the rotation is for.

Birdie bonus is the asymmetric rule

It doubles a player’s payout and never a banker’s. One golfer against three is already a lopsided position; letting the banker double three collections at once would make the role unplayable. The switch is one rule with one direction, and the row says so rather than leaving it to be discovered.

Still open

  • Whether bogey-or-better to win ships at all. Drawn as available and off — it is a common house rule but it turns a won hole into a no-pay, which is the most confusing outcome the game can produce.
  • Whether the banker announces a hole maximum or plays the round maximum every hole. Drawn as he announces, which is the traditional rule and the whole reason the role has any strategy.
