Banker, playing it

Banker is the only game in the app where money is decided before anybody swings, and the sequence in which it is decided is the entire game. Get the order wrong on screen and the format collapses into a guessing contest.

Five frames, four moments

A hole is set on the tee, doubled in the air, scored and resolved on the green — and then, before anyone moves on, the next banker has to be settled. The frames below run in that order; the first four are one hole, the fifth is a later hole where the rotation tied. The first is the one that carries the format: by the time the banker swings, every number is fixed and he is the only golfer who knows all of them.

The order is the rule

1. Banker setsA maximum for this hole, at or under the round ceiling.
2. Three betEach opponent picks his own number, floor to the banker’s max.
3. Bets lockThen, and only then, the banker tees off — last, knowing all three.
4. In the airPlayers double on their own shot. The banker counters on his, doubling everything.

Step 3 is the one the app has to enforce rather than record. A banker who can still see bets moving after his tee shot is playing a different game, so the screen has an explicit lock and the button says what it unlocks: Lock bets — Paul tees off. Nothing above the lock is editable afterwards without a visible correction.

Entering the bets is a gate, not a form

The banker’s maximum goes in first, as chips rather than a keypad — a hole max is one of four or five habitual numbers, and a stepper invites a $17 bet nobody wants to settle. The round ceiling is printed beside it so a banker cannot exceed what the group agreed at setup.

Then each opponent picks his own number, floor to the banker’s max, on his own row. The rows are independent and visible to each other: this is a table game and concealing what Dave bet from Sam would be inventing a rule the format does not have.

Max chipThe banker’s number is the top of every opponent’s range. Change it before the lock and the ranges move with it.

The lock is the only irreversible-feeling action in the game and the button says what it does rather than what it is: Lock bets — Paul tees off. A banker who can still watch bets move after his tee shot is playing a different game.

Score entry is the shared surface, and it resolves nothing

Entry itself is not a Banker screen — it is the same four-row card and inline picker every game in the app uses, centred on net par, one row active at a time. What is Banker-specific is what surrounds it, and one rule: no row resolves until all four scores are in.

The bet rows sit below holding their stakes with the word unresolved, and the banker’s worst case stays a worst case. Resolving one bet at a time would be technically possible and badly wrong: three of the four numbers are meaningless until the banker’s is among them, and a golfer watching his row flip to lost and back as scores arrive will not trust any of it. The counter reads 1 to go instead, and the button says Post the hole.

The banker’s own row is tinted and tagged, because the whole card is read against it — his net is the number the other three are compared to, and burying it in alphabetical order would make every reader hunt for it.

The card sits under the entry, in net

Same card as the leaderboard: gold is who banked, a green box beat him, a dot is a stroke received. It is cut to the holes already posted, so the hole being entered stays blank rather than showing a half-finished row.

Net rather than gross, for the reason the leaderboard gives: a hole here is three net comparisons against one net number, and gross on the card would make the reader do the subtraction four times a row to check a single bet.

Doubles are called in about four seconds

“Double” is shouted at a ball in flight. Nobody is holding a phone, so the controls are not really live — they are a fast way to record what was just shouted, and they are designed for a thumb at walking pace: one tap per player, one tap for the banker, no confirmation.

They stay open until the first score is entered, then close. That window is generous on purpose: the alternative is a golfer arguing at settlement that he called it, and the app having no row to put it in.

The banker’s counter is a single full-width control, not three toggles, because it is one decision that lands on all three bets at once. Drawing it as three would suggest he can pick.

Par 3s swap the word

On a par 3 the airborne call is a triple. The buttons change label rather than adding a fourth option — there is no double available on a par 3, so offering one would be wrong, not merely redundant.

The chain, not the total

Each resolved row shows how it got there: $10 bet · ×2 his double · ×2 counter = $40. This is the only screen in the app that shows arithmetic, and it earns it — three bets on one hole with two independent doublers produce a number no golfer can verify from memory, and the argument at the bar is always about the chain rather than the outcome.

The birdie bonus appears in the chain as a fourth link and only on a row that pays out — never on a tied row, which pays nothing, and never for the banker. That asymmetry is the rule made visible.

A tie is no action

The banker does not win ties. Match his net and the bet is void — whatever it had grown to. So a tied row reads TIED — NO ACTION with $0, and the chain still prints what the bet had reached, because a $40 bet that paid nothing is a fact the group wants to see rather than an omission.

This is what makes the banker’s counter a genuine gamble. A counter-double turns three bets into three bigger ones, and a hole where two opponents halve him collects on neither — he doubled his way into a hole worth less than his exposure suggested.

The banker’s exposure sits in his own banner

One number: what this hole can cost him at the multipliers currently standing. It updates when a double lands. He is the only golfer on the hole facing three bets, and the banner is the only place in the app where a live worst case is shown, because it is the only place one exists.

Rotation is announced, and a tie is asked about

Low net takes the role, and when it is outright the app just says so. A role that silently changed hands is the fastest way to have two golfers think they are banking the 8th.

A tie for low net is the interesting case, because the traditional tiebreak is whoever holed out first and no phone can see that. So the app does not guess: the fourth frame is the picker, on the tee, naming the tied golfers and asking the group which of them holed out first. One tap, and the next hole cannot start until it is answered — the button stays disabled reading confirm the banker.

DefaultThe group says who. The app asks; the group answers from what happened on the green.
DrawOffered on the card itself for groups who would rather not adjudicate, and selectable as the standing rule at setup. A drawn result stays changeable until a score is posted — the app’s guess must never outrank the group’s memory.
Banker keeps itThe third setup option. Simplest, and it hands the role and the exposure to whoever is playing best.

Whatever settles it, the answer is recorded with its reason — holed out first, drawn, banker kept it — and carried to the receipt. A rotation nobody can account for is how a Banker round ends in an argument about a hole three hours earlier.

Still open

  • Whether one phone runs the hole or all four do. Drawn as one, which matches the rest of the app but makes every double a relayed shout.
  • Whether a bet can be corrected after the lock. Drawn as yes, with a visible correction — refusing outright means a mistyped bet ends the game.
  • Whether the banker’s hole maximum should default to his last one. Probably yes; most bankers repeat.
  • Whether an answered tie can be corrected after the next hole has started. Drawn as no — by then bets have been set against a banker, and unwinding them is worse than living with it.
