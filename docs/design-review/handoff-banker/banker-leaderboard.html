Banker, leaderboard

Eighteen holes, three bets a hole, two independent doublers. Fifty-four one-on-ones in a round, and no golfer alive can reconstruct them. This board exists so nobody has to.

It is the round, not your round

The earlier draft opened as four golfer rows you tapped to expand, each showing that golfer's own ledger and hiding everyone else's. It answered how did I do four times over and never once answered what happened out there — and in a game where the whole foursome watched every bet get called, hiding Dave's number from Sam was inventing a privacy rule the format does not have.

So the board is one table: eighteen hole rows, four golfer columns, every cell signed. Nothing is per-viewer; four phones show the same screen.

A rowOne hole. The four cells are what that hole did to each man — and because every bet was one-on-one, the row sums to zero.
Gold cellWho banked. Reading the gold down the table shows the rotation, and it is the only marker the table needs.
DashNothing moved. Either a tie — a tie is no action — or, for a golfer who banked, a hole where the three bets cancelled.

Money, not multipliers

The table shows what each hole paid, not what was on it. An earlier pass marked multiplied bets with an outline and it made the table a puzzle: a golfer would find the loud cell and then have to work out whether the noise cost him anything. The multipliers are in the play screen's chain, hole by hole, where they can be read in full.

What survives here instead is the banking / betting split under the total, because those are two different games. Three bets against you at once is high variance by construction; one bet of your own size is not. A golfer can finish level having been wildly up as banker and wildly down as a player, and that pair is the only useful thing a Banker round says about how it was played.

The card is net, because every bet settles on net

Other games in the app show gross with a stroke dot, the way golfers write it on paper. Banker does not: a hole here is three net comparisons against one net number, and putting gross on the card would make the reader do the subtraction four times a row to check a single bet.

So the cells are net scores, and two marks carry the whole hole. Gold is the banker; a green box beat him. Everything unmarked lost to him or tied him — and since a tie is no action, unmarked does not mean paid. The money table above says who paid.

The biggest holes get named

A Banker round is nearly always settled by two or three holes where a doubled bet met good shots. Everybody half-remembers them and nobody agrees on the number, so they are printed: the hole, what happened, and the largest single move on it.

One quantity in the right-hand column, and it is named: +$120 Sam, not a bare figure. A block whose column silently alternates between the banker’s net and one opponent’s bet is unreadable, and ranking by the banker’s net would bury the round’s biggest hole — the 14th, where three tripled bets cancelled to nothing for the banker while $120 changed hands around him.

Ranking is money and nothing else

No points, no match record. The total row sorts nothing — it is a table, in hole order, and holes banked is a fact about the round rather than an achievement: the golfer who banked seven is as likely to have been beaten seven times as to have collected.

Still open

  • Whether eighteen rows is too tall for a phone. It scrolls, and the alternative — collapsing to nines — hides exactly the holes the group argues about. Drawn as all eighteen.
  • Whether a cell should open the hole's three bets in detail. Probably yes, as a sheet; it is the one place per-hole depth is worth a tap.

The banker plays each opponent one-on-one, net. Beat him and he pays your bet; lose and you pay him. A tie is no action — the banker does not win ties. Players double on their own shot; the banker’s counter doubles every standing bet. Par 3s triple instead. A player’s birdie doubles his payout — never a tie, never the banker’s. Lowest net on the hole banks the next one; a tie for low is drawn.
