Survivor on the lock screen

Survivor is the purest case for a live activity in the whole set. You can be knocked out by a shot you did not see. Three golfers are playing one hole; the moment the last card is in, one of them is out of the Survivor and out of the money on it. Nothing about that is visible to a man walking to his ball.

So this card carries news rather than arithmetic. The figures are small and countable — the reader can hold them in his head. What he cannot hold is the fact that his afternoon just changed while he was reading a putt.

The headline is a word, not a number

ALIVE or OUT. Every other card in the set opens with a value, because every other game is measured in something — holes, points, skins, dollars. Survivor is measured in whether you are still in it, and that is a word.

It takes the reader’s own state and nothing else. A count of survivors in the headline would be the group’s state, and there is a slot for that.

The state slot counts the room

3 IN over SURVIVOR 2. How many are left is the tension, and which Survivor is running says how much of the round is on this one. Both are group facts, which is why they sit opposite the personal word rather than inside it.

When the reader is out, the count changes person: 2 PLAYING. He is no longer one of them, and the card should not imply he is.

The track shows this Survivor only

One row per golfer, one cell per hole of the Survivor being played — not the round. A Survivor is two to six holes long, so the whole thing fits at a glance, and the reader can see how long this one has run and who dropped out of it on which hole.

Scoping it to the current Survivor is the whole point. A round-length track is the leaderboard’s job, where all the Survivors sit side by side; on a lock screen the only one that can still cost you money is the one you are in.

Cells read: filled survived the hole, outlined bright is the hole in play, outlined faint is a hole this Survivor has not reached, orange is the hole a golfer went out on, and empty is after that. The hole numbers across the top tie the track to the locked corner above it.

Three columns minimum. On the first hole of a Survivor there is one cell per lane, so each bar runs the full width and the track reads as three progress bars rather than holes. Holding the grid at three columns puts the cell at a third of the width and the two holes ahead beside it, faint — already the mark for a hole this Survivor has not reached. It applies to holes 1 and 2 only; from three cells the grid grows normally, and it costs no height either way.

The reader’s own row is the bright name. It is deliberately not a colour — the accent on this card belongs to the headline word.

Names, because three is a small number

The sides line lists who is in and who is out, in full. A four-man group is people rather than positions — the same argument the Sixes and Nassau cards make for naming pairs.

Out is dimmed rather than coloured. There is one accent per card and it belongs to the headline; a red name would read as an alert on a fact that has already settled.

Money settles per Survivor

Each Survivor is its own pot — $5 a Survivor — so money lands two or three times a round rather than once at the end. The slot fills the moment a Survivor closes and holds its running total after: +$10.

Never a $0. Before the first Survivor closes the slot is empty, not zero.

Three golfers at five dollars means a win is +$10 and a loss is −$5, so the running figure only moves in those steps and never falls back. The cards here are one timeline: Survivor 1 won (+$10), Survivor 2 lost (+$5), Survivor 3 running, and three of five won by the last green (+$20). A reader who adds them up gets the same number the final card shows.

What gets a push

OutYes. The reader lost money on a hole he may not have seen finish.
Survivor wonYes, and it is the good one. A pot closed in his favour.
Down to twoYes. The next hole can end it.
New SurvivorNo. He is standing on the tee it starts from.

Open questions

A Survivor that reaches 18 unresolved. No blood, nothing carried. The card has no state for it yet and the final would need to say so plainly rather than reading like a loss.

Groups larger than four. The names line is the ceiling. Five or more wants a count and no names, which changes the card’s character.
