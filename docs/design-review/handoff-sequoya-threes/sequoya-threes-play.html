Sequoya 3s, playing it

A three-hole match is over almost as soon as it starts. Two holes decide it, which means the press is not a garnish here — it is the mechanism that keeps the third hole worth playing. Everything on this screen is arranged around that.

The banner carries the wager, because the wager moves

Sixes can put the stake in a quiet corner: it never changes. Here a second bet opens mid-match without anyone touching the phone, so the banner is a list of the live bets and nothing else — one line each: name, hole range, amount, state. The pairings are named on the score cards immediately below, so carrying them in the banner too was two lines of nothing.

The auto press announces itself and asks nothing

It fires on a won first hole and there is no decision in it, so it gets a row and no dialogue. A press here is not a doubling — it is a new bet at the same amount, running over the holes that are left, which is why it gets its own line with its own state rather than a multiplier on the match. The one thing it must not do is arrive silently: money that appears without a visible cause is the fastest way to lose a group’s trust in the app.

The press is a button before it is a card

The trailing side has to be able to press without waiting to be asked, so the button is the permanent home of the action — it sits above the hole header and lights amber the moment a press is legal: after a halved first hole, or from the second hole of the match onward. Unlit the rest of the time, with the reason on the second line.

It names the hole it would cover, not the match, and it is called your press. Both exist to keep it separable from the auto press already in the banner — two $5 bets over the same holes with the same label would be unreadable at settlement, so a hand-called press starts on the next hole and carries the caller’s name.

The full amber card only appears when the app is the one asking — a match that has just closed with a hole still to play. The button dims and points at it, because the same decision must not have two live controls.

The offered press is a different object and gets a different colour

Amber, not green. It is the only thing on this screen that expires — it is live until the third tee and then it is gone — and the only thing addressed to two of the four golfers rather than all of them. Both facts are printed on it.

Why a press is a bet and not a multiplier

A pair that loses holes 1 and 2 has lost the match — two down with one to play cannot be recovered. Doubling a wager you have already lost is not a press, it is a donation, and no golfer would ever tap it.

So a press is a fresh bet at the same amount over the holes that remain, settled on its own. The auto press covers holes 2 and 3; a hand-called press covers whatever is left when it is called. The match bet closes and pays on its own schedule, which is why this screen shows two live lines rather than one line with a multiplier on it.

Best ball, marked in the cell

Each pair’s counting net is filled green. Two rows, one highlight, and the comparison the match runs on is readable without arithmetic — which matters more in this game than most, because the net that counts changes hands hole to hole and a partner who thinks he carried the match will say so at settlement.

The card at the bottom, holes played only

Under the entry cards sits the same scorecard the leaderboard shows, cut to the holes that have scores. It is the answer to what did he make on 4, which is asked on every tee, and it is where the boxed net makes the best-ball comparison checkable rather than asserted.

It is last on the screen deliberately. Scoring happens at the top, in the thumb zone; the card is reference and can be scrolled to.

Three cells, not eighteen — and one row, not a card

The by-hole strip is only this match, and three holes do not earn a card with a heading. It collapses to a single row: label on the left, three small cells on the right. The round’s other five matches are a list of results underneath, not a scorecard — they are settled money, and money reads better as a column than as a grid.

Every partner twice

Matches 4, 5 and 6 repeat 1, 2 and 3 in order, so the match list is a natural pair of columns — the same pairing on two lines, nine holes apart. A golfer checking whether he has had a bad day or a bad partner can read it off the list, which is a question this format invites and no other format in the app does.

Still open

  • Whether a halved three-hole match should roll into the next one or simply pay nothing. Drawn as pays nothing — carryover across a pairing change means a wager owed by a pair that no longer exists.
  • Whether the group can re-draw the order at the turn. Drawn as no: the repeat is the point of the format.
