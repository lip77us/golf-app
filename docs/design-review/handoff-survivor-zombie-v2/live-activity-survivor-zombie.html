The Zombie option on the lock screen

Plain Survivor has one direction of travel: you are in until you are out. Zombie makes it reversible. A knocked-out golfer who goes low outright on a later hole is alive again, and whoever loses that hole takes his place in Zombieville.

That turns the card from a status into a standing offer. A man who is out still has a reason to look at his phone, which is not true of any other card in this set.

ZOMBIE is a third headline word

Plain Survivor has two: ALIVE and OUT. Here OUT is never used, because nobody is out while the Survivor runs — they are in the seat. The three words become ALIVE, ZOMBIE and BACK IN.

BACK IN is the only headline in the set that describes something that just happened rather than a standing state. It holds for one hole and then reverts to ALIVE, because after that it is no longer news.

The plum is the same colour, lightened for a dark card

Zombieville is plum everywhere in Halved — the setup toggle, the play screen chip, the leaderboard rail. On a lock screen the token itself is too dark to read against the glass, so this card uses a lifted plum and nothing else changes: same meaning, same position in the vocabulary.

It is the third accent on a card that normally allows one. That is deliberate and bounded: mint for alive, orange never appears, plum for the seat. A reader who sees plum knows exactly one thing is true.

The track keeps the Zombie on it

One row per golfer, one cell per hole of the Survivor in play. Plain Survivor empties a golfer’s row after he goes out; here it cannot, because a Zombie is still hitting shots — that is the entire mechanic.

So the seat gets two cells of its own: solid plum is the hole that sent him there, and plum outline is every hole he plays from the seat. A return is a solid white cell — the brightest mark on the track, and the only one that is not a shade of something else.

White rather than mint on purpose. Mint is the reader’s colour on this card — ALIVE, BACK IN, his own good news — and a return lands on whichever row it happened to, so mint would mean the reader’s good fortune on one row and his opponent’s on the next. White says the round turned here and leaves who it turned for to the row it sits in.

Read together, the two plum states answer the question the headline cannot: not just that he is in the seat, but how many swings he has had at getting out of it.

The state slot carries the offer

LOW GETS YOU IN. Not you are out — the reader knows that from the headline. The slot says what to do about it, which is the only actionable line on any card in the set.

It is not a forecast. It states the rule, not a probability of clearing it.

A long Survivor is where the track earns its place

Two-hole Survivors need no picture — the headline and the state slot say everything. A five-hole Survivor is the case the track exists for: it has usually changed hands, and the reader cannot reconstruct that from a single sentence.

Five cells is the practical ceiling at this width. Beyond that the cells fall under 40px and the hole numbers start to crowd, which is the point at which the card should stop drawing holes and say how many instead — a Survivor that long is a fact about the round rather than about the hole.

The handover is named

When the reader comes back in, somebody else goes to Zombieville, and the card names him: SAM TO ZOMBIEVILLE. A return that did not say who paid for it would read as a gift.

One timeline, and it adds up

Three golfers at five dollars: a Survivor won is +$10, one lost is −$5. The cards on this sheet are a single round of six Survivors — 1 won, 2 lost, 3 won from the seat, 4 and 5 lost, 6 won on the 18th. The slot therefore reads +$5 while Survivor 3 is still running, +$10 inside Survivor 5, +$5 again on the 18th, and +$15 once the last one lands.

The seat history is single-threaded too: one man in Zombieville at a time. When it is somebody else the state slot names him; when it is the reader the headline word does that job and the slot carries the offer instead. Two cards never disagree about who holds it, which is the thing a reader would notice first and trust least.

What gets a push

To ZombievilleYes. It is the moment the offer opens.
Back inYes, and it is the loudest push in the set.
Zombie wins itYes. A Zombie who takes the Survivor on 18 kills its pot — everyone needs that.
Still the ZombieNo. Nothing changed.

Open questions

A Zombie win on 18. The rule kills that Survivor’s payment. Money going to nobody has no state on this card yet, and it cannot read like a loss for the winner.

Two men in Zombieville. Larger groups can send more than one. The seat is drawn as singular throughout — the headline word, the handover line and the plum square all assume one occupant.
