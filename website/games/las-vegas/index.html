How to play Las Vegas

Two teams of two, and instead of adding your scores you stand them next to each other. A four and a five is not nine, it is forty-five. The low number wins the difference, which is how one bad hole turns into fifty points.

THE SHORT ANSWER

Each team combines its two scores into a two-digit number, lower score first — a 4 and a 5 make 45. Compare the two teams’ numbers; the low one wins, and the margin is the difference between them, played for cents rather than dollars a point. Any birdie flips the other team’s number, so their 45 becomes 54.

The rules

Split the four of you into two teams before you tee off. Both golfers on a team play their own ball all the way out — there is no best ball and nothing to concede. At the end of the hole each team makes a number out of its two scores, and the two numbers are compared.

The margin is the difference between the numbers, not one point for the hole. That is the whole character of the game: a routine hole is worth eight or ten points, and a hole where somebody loses a ball is worth fifty.

1
Pick the teams
Two and two, drawn or agreed. Most groups keep them for the round; some redraw at the turn.
2
Everybody holes out
Both scores count on every hole, which means the second ball matters as much as the first.
3
Make the number, take the difference
Low score first, apply any flip, subtract. The points go into a running total for the round.

One rule, and one exception that comes up more than you would think.

A ten or worse goes in front. A 6 and a 10 is 106, not 610 — otherwise a single blow-up hole would be worth six hundred points and the round would be over.
Why the second ball is the whole game
In a best-ball game your partner making seven costs you nothing at all. Here it goes straight onto the back of your number. A team that goes 4 and 7 makes 47; the same team with a 4 and a 5 makes 45. Nobody is carried in Las Vegas, and every shot the second ball drops is a point off the hole.

The flip

A birdie by anybody on your team reverses the other team’s number. Their 47 becomes 74. Nothing changes about your own number — you just make theirs as expensive as it can be. This is what turns Las Vegas from arithmetic into a gambling game, and it is the rule most groups get wrong on the first tee.

Birdie
Flip the opponents’ number once. Two birdies on the same team flip it once, not twice — the number is already the wrong way round.

Eagle
Flip, then double the whole hole. Groups differ on whether an eagle flips a number a birdie already flipped; the tidy answer is that the number is flipped once and the doubling does the rest.

The order matters
Build both numbers first, then apply the flips, then subtract. Doing it the other way round — reversing the digits as you write them down — is how a hole ends up scored twice and nobody can work out why the totals do not agree at the turn.

Picking a stake

The one thing to get right before you tee off. Points in Las Vegas run an order of magnitude higher than in any other golf game, so the per-point number has to be an order of magnitude lower. Ten cents a point is the standard.

House rules worth agreeing on the first tee

Las Vegas has more variants than most, because every group finds its own way to cap the damage. Settle these before you start.

Does a birdie flip the number, or multiply it?
Two ways to price a birdie, and they are not close in effect. Flip reverses the opponents’ number — 58 becomes 85 — so the swing depends on how lopsided their two scores were: a 58 flips savagely, a 55 not at all. Multiply doubles the hole’s point difference instead, which is predictable and always hurts. Flip is the traditional rule and the more interesting one, because it rewards a birdie most when the other side already has one bad score on the hole.
Which handicap method?
Three choices. Gross is the traditional game and right when the four are close in ability. Net gives everyone their full allocation. Strokes off low is the middle road and the usual pick: the lowest handicap plays to scratch and everyone else receives the difference, allocated by stroke index. A Net % setting scales whichever allocation you chose — 100% is the full difference, and dialling it down is how a group softens a wide handicap spread without going gross. Whichever you pick, strokes matter more here than in most games: a stroke does not just win a hole, it changes a digit. Turning a 6 into a 5 moves your number by ten points if it is the back digit, and by far more if it reorders the pair.
Does a halved hole carry?
By default nothing happens: the difference is zero and the hole is simply halved. With carryover on, a halved hole’s stake rides onto the next one, so the hole after an identical pair of numbers is worth double. It makes the rare halve matter and it is the one option that can make a quiet stretch expensive.
Is there a maximum score on a hole?
Most groups cap an individual at double par, or at triple bogey, and write that number down instead of what actually happened. Without a cap, one lost ball on a par five can be worth more than the rest of the front nine put together. Capping is the single most common house rule in the game — and it is an agreement between the four of you, not a setting: agree the number on the first tee and post the capped score.
Do the teams change?
Fixed teams for eighteen is simplest. The livelier version rotates every six holes so everyone partners everyone, and the round becomes three separate matches — which also means nobody spends the whole day tied to the one man who is out of form.
Does an eagle really double?
Some groups double, some triple, some do nothing beyond the flip. Doubling is the common one. Whatever you pick, say it out loud on the first tee, because the hole it comes up on is always a big one.

Questions people ask

Why is it called Las Vegas?

Because of the swing. Points come in twos and threes on the ordinary holes and then fifty at once on the hole where somebody makes eight, which is a good deal more like a casino than a golf bet. The name is also a warning about the stake.

Can you play Las Vegas with three or six golfers?

It needs even teams, so four or six. With six you make three-digit numbers, which sounds absurd and plays fine as long as the cap is tight. With three there is no version that works; play Wolf instead.

What happens if both teams make the same number?

Nothing. The difference is zero, so the hole is halved and no points change hands. It is rarer than it sounds, because the two numbers have to match on both digits.

Should we cap the hole score?

Yes, unless everybody in the group is a single-figure handicap. Double par is the usual cap, agreed between the group and posted as the score rather than set as an option. Without one, Las Vegas stops being a game of golf and becomes a game about who has the worst hole, and the man who has it stops enjoying his round.
