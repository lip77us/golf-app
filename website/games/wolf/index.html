How to play Wolf

Four golfers, and every hole one of them has to decide something on the tee: take a partner, or take on all three alone for triple. It is the best four-man betting game there is, and the only hard part is keeping score.

THE SHORT ANSWER

On each hole one golfer is the Wolf, taken from a rotation set before the round. The Wolf tees off last. After watching the other three drive, the Wolf either picks a partner for a two-against-two best-ball hole, or goes Lone Wolf against all three for triple points. Win the hole, take the points; lose it, pay them.

The rules

Before you tee off, put the four of you in an order. That order is the rotation, and it never changes: first golfer is the Wolf on hole 1, second on hole 2, and so on round and round. Over sixteen holes everyone gets four turns.

The Wolf tees off last. That is the whole engine of the game — he stands on the tee having seen three drives, knowing exactly what is on offer, and has to commit before anyone plays a second shot.

The three choices

Everything the Wolf can do on a tee, and what each one is worth. The point values below are the common ones; groups change them all the time.

Take a partner
The safe play, and the one that happens most. The Wolf names one of the other three and it becomes a two-against-two best-ball hole. Both sides count their better net score.

The one rule people forget
The pick has to be made before the next shot is played. You cannot walk up the fairway, see how the lies turned out, and then choose. Once someone has hit an approach, the Wolf is committed to whatever he said on the tee — or to going alone if he said nothing.

Scoring, worked all the way through

Holes 17 and 18

Four golfers into eighteen holes does not divide. The rotation covers sixteen, and the two that are left over go to whoever is losing: last place is the Wolf on 17, and last place after 17 is the Wolf on 18.

Treat that as the game rather than a variant. A rotation that just runs out leaves the man who is down with two holes and nothing to play for, and the closing stretch is exactly where a Wolf game should have something at stake. Being handed the wolf twice, with a triple available on both, is a real way back — and it is why nobody in a Wolf game is ever out of it walking off the sixteenth.

House rules worth agreeing on the first tee

Wolf has been played a hundred ways. None of these is wrong. Settle them before you tee off, because every one of them changes who wins.

Does the Wolf pick after each drive, or after all three?
The tighter old version has the Wolf teeing off first and deciding on each golfer the instant his ball lands — pass on a good drive and it is gone for good. Letting the Wolf hit last and choose from all three is friendlier and quicker, and it is the more common game now. The old way produces far more Lone Wolves.
What happens on a tie?
Simplest is that a halved hole pays nobody, which is what most groups do. Some make the Wolf lose ties, on the grounds that he had the pick and the advantage that comes with it. That one bites hardest on a Lone Wolf, where a tie turns into a triple loss.
Should a clean win by the other three pay double?
The Wolf has the pick, so beating him without it is the harder thing to do. A clean-win bonus pays the non-Wolf side double when they win outright, which pushes back against a Wolf who only ever takes the best drive. It also makes a bad pick expensive twice over, so try it for a round before adopting it.
Does everybody have to go solo at least once?
Left alone, a cautious golfer can take a partner on all four of his turns and never risk anything. Requiring each golfer to go Lone or Blind once — in practice, locking the partner option out of his last turn if he has not — guarantees four solo holes a round. It is the single best rule for a group where nobody ever takes the shot.
Handicaps, or straight up?
Gross is simplest and favours the good player. Strokes off the low man is the better way to handle handicaps here: the best player gets nothing, everyone else plays the difference, and those strokes fall on the hardest holes. It matters more in Wolf than in most games, because a stroke is what makes a high handicapper worth picking.
Is there a bonus for beating a Lone Wolf?
Some groups pay the pack double when they beat a Wolf who went alone, which makes going alone genuinely brave rather than just expensive. Others cap what any one player can lose over the round, which keeps a bad day from turning into a real one.

Scoring Wolf without doing the arithmetic

Wolf is the hardest of the common golf games to keep on paper, and it is worth being honest about why. Every hole you have to remember whose turn it was, what he chose, which side won it after strokes, and then push a different number into four different columns — three of them negative. Miss one hole and the total is wrong for the rest of the day.

Questions people ask

Can you play Wolf with three golfers?

Yes, though it changes the game — the Wolf picks from two, and going alone is one against two rather than one against three, so the triple is usually cut to double. The rotation covers eighteen holes exactly with three, which does mean no catch-up holes at the end.

What is a good stake for Wolf?

A dollar a point is the usual starting place. Worth knowing before you agree to it: the worst realistic day is losing every hole as the pack and getting caught on a few Lone Wolves, which lands somewhere near thirty times the point value. If that number is uncomfortable, halve the stake rather than the fun.

When should you actually go Lone Wolf?

Two situations. Your ball is stiff on a par three and nobody else is inside twenty feet. Or you are down late, the arithmetic says a partner hole will not catch you up, and the triple is the only thing that will. Going alone because all three drives were poor is the common mistake — you still have to beat the best of three balls, and one of them will make par.

Is Wolf the same as Hollywood or Sixes?

No, though they get confused. In the partnerships are fixed for six holes at a time and everyone eventually partners everyone. In Wolf the teams change every hole and one golfer chooses them. Wolf is the one with the gamble in it.
