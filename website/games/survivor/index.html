How to play Survivor

The three-ball game. Worst score on the hole is knocked out, the two left standing play for the money, and the moment it is settled a fresh one starts on the next tee. And under the Zombie rule the man you just buried can climb back out.

THE SHORT ANSWER

Three golfers all ante the stake. On each hole the worst net score is eliminated; the two who are left play the next hole as a decider, and the lower score takes the pot. Then everyone is back in and the next Survivor begins — up to nine of them in a round.

The rules

Survivor is the answer to the three-ball. Most golf bets want an even number of players; this one only works with an odd one, because the whole game is that somebody has to be the worst.

All three ante the stake into a pot. From there each Survivor runs in two phases, and it takes at least two holes to settle one.

Everyone posts a net score and the high man is knocked out of this Survivor. He keeps playing his own round; he just cannot win this pot.

Settle a Survivor and the next one starts on the very next hole, all three back in. A clean round of two-hole Survivors gets you nine of them.

Handicaps are worth settling before you start. Playing strokes off the low man is the usual choice: the best player gets nothing, the other two get the difference, and those strokes fall on the hardest holes of the round rather than being spread across a Survivor — nobody knows how long one will last until it ends.

Ties, and what happens on 18

Ties are where three-player games usually fall apart. Survivor handles them by never resolving anything it cannot resolve cleanly — it just runs on to the next hole.

Two worst scores tie
Nobody goes out. All three stay in and the next hole does the eliminating instead.
The decider is halved
The pot carries. The same two play on until one of them is lower, so a Survivor can stretch four or five holes.
The eighteenth
There is no room to eliminate and then decide, so the last hole settles whatever is standing. With all three still in, low ball takes it and any tie for low is no blood. With two left, it is the decider. And a Survivor that reaches 18 without resolving pays nothing — no blood, nothing carried forward.

The Zombie rule

On by default, and the best thing in the game. The eliminated player stays on the card through the decider. He is a Zombie: dead, but still posting a score, and still able to do damage.

Win the decider hole outright — lower than both men who put you out — and you are alive again. Somebody has to take your place.

Low outright, deciders tie
Nobody to send out, so nobody goes. All three are alive again and the Survivor resets to the elimination phase.

Tied or worse
Nothing changes. The hole settles between the two deciders as normal, and the Zombie stays dead.
A tie is no good to a Zombie. He has to be alone at the bottom of the hole, which is the point — coming back should cost a putt that matters.

One consequence worth knowing on the first tee: with Zombie on, a Survivor can run a very long way. If it is still alive on the eighteenth it pays nothing, and a Zombie who wins the last hole kills that Survivor's payment outright. Nobody is ever safely ahead, which is either the appeal or the reason your group leaves it off.

House rules worth agreeing on the first tee

Zombie on, or turn it off?
On is the default and the livelier game: longer Survivors, and nobody safely ahead. Turning it off makes Survivor short and predictable — most settle in two holes and everyone knows where they stand. Decide once, for the round; switching halfway is how arguments start.
Net, gross, or strokes off the low man?
Strokes off the low man is the fairest of the three in a mixed group, and it is what makes a high handicapper dangerous on a hard hole. Gross is simplest and favours the best player, which in a three-ball he will notice.
What is a sensible stake?
The worst case is losing every Survivor, so the exposure is the stake times the number that can settle — nine at most over eighteen holes. At $5 that is $45. Pick the stake off the number you would not enjoy paying, not off the number per hole.
Do you carry an unsettled Survivor?
The standard is no: one that reaches the eighteenth alive pays nothing. Some groups instead split its pot between whoever was still standing, which rewards surviving rather than winning. Both work; only one of them ends the round quietly.

Scoring Survivor without losing track of who is dead

The arithmetic in Survivor is easy. The bookkeeping is not. Which Survivor are we on, who is out of it, is this hole an elimination or a decider, did that tie stop the clock, and — with Zombie on — is the man on the green over there technically back in. Three golfers will remember three different answers.

Questions people ask

How many players do you need for Survivor?

Three. Survivor is the standard answer to an odd group, because the game needs somebody to be the worst score on the hole. Four can play a version where two go out at once, but it is a different game and it settles far quicker.

What is the Zombie rule in Survivor?

The rule that keeps the eliminated player on the card. He posts a score through the decider hole, and if it is lower than both deciders outright he is alive again — the higher of the two takes his place. Ties do not count; the Zombie has to be alone at the bottom of the hole.

What happens if the two worst scores tie?

Nobody is eliminated. All three stay in and the next hole does the eliminating instead. The same principle covers a halved decider: the pot carries and the same two play on until one is lower.

Does an unfinished Survivor pay out on 18?

No. A Survivor still running on the eighteenth settles what is standing on that hole — and if it cannot settle, it pays nothing. Nothing is carried forward to a following round.

How much can you lose playing Survivor?

The stake times the number of Survivors that settle, nine at most in eighteen holes. At $5 a Survivor the worst possible day is $45, and that requires losing every single one.
