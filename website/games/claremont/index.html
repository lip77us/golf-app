How to play Claremont

A Nassau for a foursome that wants every ball to matter. The top game is an ordinary better-ball Nassau. The bottom game counts both balls on every hole, so a partner who has a bad hole still has a point to play for, and the side that is losing on top can be winning underneath.

THE SHORT ANSWER

Claremont is two Nassaus at once, two against two. On top, the lower best ball wins the hole. On the bottom, every hole is worth 2 points: one for the lower low ball and one for the lower second ball. Each game has its own front, back and overall, so a “$10 Claremont” is six bets of $10. A tied bet is a push, and presses fire by themselves: on top at 2 down, on the bottom at 4 points down.

The rules

Four golfers in two fixed sides, net scores. On every hole each side has a low ball (its better net score) and a second ball (its other one). Both games are played from those same four scores at the same time, and you agree one stake that every bet is worth.

Each game is a Nassau: a front nine, a back nine and an overall eighteen, each its own bet. The top is counted in holes, the bottom in points, and the side ahead when a bet runs out wins it. A tied bet pays nothing — no countback, nothing rolls forward.

Low ball against low ball. The lower one wins the hole; a tie halves it. Plain better-ball match play.

The bottom
Two points a hole: one for the lower low ball, one for the lower second ball. A tied pair of balls scores nothing.
3
Six bets
Front, back and overall on top, and the same three on the bottom. Every one is worth the stake.
The one rule people forget
The two games are scored separately, even though they come from the same four scores. Winning the hole on top does not mean winning it on the bottom: a side can take the hole with its low ball and still split the bottom 1–1, or lose it 0–2 because both its balls were worse. And as in any Nassau, each overall is its own eighteen-hole count, not the two nines added up.

The press

A press is a new bet over the holes left in a nine, opened for the side that is losing. The original bet carries on exactly as it was. In Claremont the presses are always on and automatic: nobody asks for one, nobody can refuse one, and nobody has to remember.

Each game has its own trigger, because the bottom moves twice as fast. Four points is two holes’ worth on the bottom, the same distance as two holes down on top. Each press runs to the end of its nine and is worth the stake.

On top, at two down
The moment a side goes two holes down in a nine on the low-ball game, a press opens over the rest of that nine. The same rule as an automatic Nassau.

What a press is worth
The stake, the same as every other bet.
On a $10 Claremont every press is $10. Six base bets is already $60 of exposure, and with two games pressing on their own a bad nine can open more. Pick the stake with the presses in mind, because there is no press to decline.

A tied bet

All square on top, or level on points underneath, and that bet is simply a push: no money changes hands, no countback, nothing carried into the next nine. It happens to one of the six bets most rounds, and more often on top than on the bottom, because points rarely finish exactly level.

A tied hole works the same way. Equal low balls halve the hole on top and leave the low-ball point unscored on the bottom; equal second balls leave the second point unscored. Neither one carries over.

Worth knowing before you tee off

Claremont has fewer choices than a Nassau, on purpose. These are the ones left, and the ones groups ask about.

Why are the presses not optional?
Because the two triggers are what make the game. Two games pressing on their own keep both sides playing every ball to the 18th. If your group wants a quieter day, play a Nassau with presses off instead.
Handicaps, or straight up?
Gross is simplest and favours the good player. Strokes off the low man is the better way to play handicaps in match play: the best player gets nothing, everyone else plays the difference, and the strokes land on the hardest holes. In Claremont a stroke counts twice, once on top and once on the bottom, so set the handicap with that in mind.
How much can the day cost?
Six times the stake on the base bets, plus a stake for every press. There is no cap setting, so the stake is the lever: a $5 Claremont is a lively day, a $20 one is a serious one.
Can a twosome or threesome play it?
No. The bottom needs two balls a side, so Claremont is always two against two. A twosome plays a Nassau; a threesome plays a Triple Nassau. Want both balls to count as well? That is Claremont, a second Nassau on the same four scores.

Scoring Claremont without doing the arithmetic

Questions people ask

What does a $10 Claremont cost?

Sixty dollars if a side loses all six base bets, plus $10 for every press it loses. Presses are automatic in both games, so there is no fixed ceiling: choose the stake with that in mind.

What is Claremont in golf?

A four-ball game that plays two Nassaus from the same scores. The top is a better-ball Nassau in holes. The bottom gives two points a hole, one to the side with the lower low ball and one to the side with the lower second ball, and runs its own front, back and overall.

Why does the bottom press at four down and not two?

Because the bottom moves twice as fast. With two points on every hole, four points is the same distance as two holes on top. Pressing at two points would open a press after a single bad hole.

How is it different from a Nassau?

The top game is a Nassau. Claremont adds the bottom game, which makes it four players only, twice the bets, and presses that are always on. A Nassau can be played by two or four, with presses off, by hand or automatic, and with a cap.
